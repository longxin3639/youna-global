## 6. Verdict on the mini-stories

The writer was asked for two to three short first-hand mini-stories. Three landed. All three are specific and all three are tied to the surrounding advice rather than dropped in as decoration.

- **Nadia (Toronto homeware importer, Phase 2): landed, with one flaw.** The detail is strong: named city, named category, the Phase 2 entrance, 40 unattributable catalogs, two missed booths that matched her order size. This is a real cautionary tale and it earns its place in the intro. The flaw is the last two sentences, where a genuine failure is capped with a success ("she still closed a supplier two weeks later") plus a moral ("She did it the hard way") that contradicts the caution. Fix with edit 4 above so the lesson is clean.

- **Tom (Amazon seller, 300 photos): landed, and it is the best of the three.** It sits directly under the photography advice, opens with a concrete number (300 product photos), describes the fix, and closes with a benefit (follow-up takes an evening, not a week). This is exactly how a mini-story should pay off the point above it. Keep as is.

- **Daniel (kitchenware buyer, Melbourne): landed on facts, weak on framing.** The numbers are excellent (eight suppliers contacted, six replies, three sensible quotes, two trial orders) and they prove the follow-up window better than any paragraph could. The problem is the setup line, "showed how much the handoff matters", which sounds like a rubric, and the phrase "spent the changeover resting", which is slightly muddled. Fix with edit 10 above.

Overall verdict: the mini-stories did land and should stay. The one risk is that all three share an identical template, a named buyer, a home city, tidy numbers, and a tidy resolution. Three case studies built from the same mold is itself a mild AI signature, and the article's own opening warns that the fair is messy. Recommendation: keep all three, but vary at least one so the pattern breaks. Two options: (a) drop the name on one and make it a first-person memory ("I once did exactly this on my own first trip..."), or (b) let one story end without a neat win, which fits the cautionary tone of the intro and the Common First-Timer Mistakes section. Option (b) is the stronger editorial choice and costs nothing.
