# Sample Evaluation Guide: What to Check Before Placing a Bulk Order

A client once forwarded us photos of a sample that looked perfect: clean stitching, correct logo, the right weight in the hand. He paid the 30% deposit, the factory ran 2,000 units, and three weeks later the container landed with a different Pantone, a softer alloy on the hinge, and barcodes that wouldn't scan at his 3PL. The root cause was not a dishonest factory — it was a sample evaluation that stopped at "looks good." This is the single most expensive mistake we see new importers make, and it almost always traces back to one skipped step: a real **sample evaluation** before you commit to the **bulk order**.

Below is the exact checklist we use on the ground in Guangdong. It is written for buyers who already have a supplier in hand and a sample on the desk — and who want to know, specifically, what to check before the money leaves their account.

## Why Sample Evaluation Is Your Last Cheap Chance to Fix Things

A defect caught in a single sample costs you one revision, maybe a replaced component, sometimes a few days. The same defect caught after 2,000 units are boxed and on a boat costs you air freight, a rework line, a missed launch date, and sometimes the entire margin on the order.

Here is the math buyers forget:

- **Sample-stage fix:** 1 piece, 1 round of feedback, $0–$50 in revised tooling or material.
- **Bulk-stage fix:** 2,000 pieces, container return or local rework, $2,000–$20,000 in recovery cost, plus the time you can't get back.

We have watched a $4,500 order turn into a $0 payday because the buyer approved a sample by photo and never measured it. The factory was not trying to cheat — the buyer simply never told them, in measurable terms, what "right" looked like. Sample evaluation is how you define "right" before it is too late.

> **Key point:** The sample is the only physical proof you have that a factory can build what you agreed to on paper. Skip the evaluation and you are mass-producing on trust alone.

The painful part is that recovery usually costs more than the order's profit. A $5,000 order at a 20% margin leaves you $1,000 to work with — and a single container return or local rework line burns that in a day. Sample evaluation is the step that keeps the margin in your pocket, and it is also step six of our [8-step factory verification walkthrough](blog-verify-chinese-factory-audit-checklist.html): verify the factory, then verify the sample.

## Know the 3 Sample Types (Most Buyers Mix Them Up)

Suppliers will offer you several "samples," and confusing them is one of the most common first-timer errors. They are not interchangeable. We have watched buyers approve a reference sample pulled from a showroom, then receive bulk that came off a different line entirely — same drawing, different reality. Knowing exactly which sample you are holding decides whether your evaluation means anything at all.

### Reference / Fitting Samples
These confirm the rough concept and fit early in development. They are often pulled from existing production or a showroom shelf. Useful for shape and idea, useless as a quality benchmark — the factory may never build your order on that exact line.

### Pre-Production Sample (PPS) — the one to scrutinize
The PPS is made on the **actual production line, with the actual tooling and materials** that will be used for your order. This is the sample to evaluate hardest. It is your last checkpoint before mass production starts. If the PPS is wrong, you fix it now — cheaply.

### Production Samples
These are pulled from the live run itself, usually for final confirmation. By the time you see them, design changes are expensive or impossible. Use them to confirm the PPS standard held, not to discover new problems.

> **Key point:** Always approve a PPS, not a reference sample, before you release the bulk order. Ask the factory explicitly: "Is this made on the production line with final tooling?" If the answer is vague, you do not have a PPS yet.

## The 5-Zone Sample Evaluation Checklist

Work through these five zones in order, and document each with photos and written notes. A focused 20–30 minute review now saves weeks of rework later. Do this even when the product feels simple — a $3 item fails the same five tests as a $300 one. Keep a shared, dated photo log per supplier so the factory sees precisely what you measured and where it drifted; ambiguity at this stage is what a production team quietly rounds in its own favor. For the production-stage version of this same discipline once bulk begins, pair it with our [QC inspection checklist](blog-qc-inspection-checklist.html).

### Zone 1 — Dimensions & Tolerances (measure, don't eyeball)
Get a caliper or measuring tape and check every dimension on your spec sheet. For a bag, that means height, width, depth, strap length. For electronics, the housing, port placement, and weight. Even a 1 cm deviation can break fit, function, or resale.

Decide your tolerance **before** the sample arrives. A few millimeters is often acceptable; beyond your agreed range gets flagged in writing. Typical acceptable ranges run about ±1–5% depending on the product and material.

### Zone 2 — Materials & Components (BOM cross-check)
Confirm the fabric, plastic grade, metal alloy, or fill material matches what was quoted. This is where cost-cutting substitutions show up most. If you specified solid brass and the sample feels light, question it. For critical products, ask for a material certificate or test report (OEKO-TEX for textiles, a CDF for electronics).

One technique we use: physically disassemble the production-ready sample and compare every internal component against your Bill of Materials. That is how you catch a factory swapping a high-grade capacitor for a generic one — the kind of bait-and-switch that only appears after thousands of units ship.

### Zone 3 — Workmanship & Construction
The sample matches the spec. Great. Is it well made? Inspect stitching, seams, edges, and assembly. Are stitches straight, even, and tight? Are seams finished to prevent fraying? For anything worn or handled, bend it, stretch it gently, operate every zipper and button ten times. A well-constructed sample lasts longer and returns less.

### Zone 4 — Function & Durability (stress test)
Test the product the way a customer would, then push harder. For mechanical or electronic items, run them repeatedly. For anything with a battery or moving part, cycle it beyond normal use. A sample that overheats on the fifth cycle will not survive a customer's first week.

### Zone 5 — Packaging & Destination-Market Labeling
Check retail packaging, inner cartons, and inserts. Confirm barcodes scan correctly and branding matches your artwork file exactly. Then check the labeling your **destination market** requires — not just a generic mark aimed at a different region. A CE mark aimed at Europe does not clear Singapore's IMDA or Malaysia's SIRIM, and a sample that passes one market's rules does not automatically clear its neighbor's. Our [QC inspection checklist](blog-qc-inspection-checklist.html) covers how we verify this at the production stage.

> **The 5-zone quick list:**
> 1. **Dimensions** — measure against spec, apply agreed tolerance
> 2. **Materials** — BOM cross-check, request certs where relevant
> 3. **Workmanship** — stitching, seams, edges, stress points
> 4. **Function** — cycle it like a real user, then harder
> 5. **Packaging & labeling** — scan barcodes, match destination rules
>
> **AQL at sample stage:** Classify issues as *critical* (safety/legal — reject), *major* (function or marketability — usually reject), or *minor* (aesthetic — judge by volume). Defining these early stops a factory from downplaying a systemic error as "just a small variation."

## Set Your Tolerances Before, Not After

The biggest amateur move is approving a sample by feel and arguing about millimeters later. Your technical pack or spec sheet is the master document. Compare the sample against it item by item:

- Is the length exactly as specified?
- Is the zipper the one you chose?
- Are dimensions correct to the millimeter where it matters?

Transform "it looks good" into "it matches the spec." This is what separates an objective evaluation from a gamble. For products that need to fit the body or stack in a carton, inaccurate sizing is a leading cause of returns — and returns are where import margins go to die. This discipline is also what turns a one-time [factory audit](blog-verify-chinese-factory-audit-checklist.html) into a repeatable standard instead of a photo op the line forgets by next week.

> **Key point:** Any deviation, no matter how small, must be identified and corrected at the sample stage. A mistake caught on one piece is a simple fix. The same mistake on 2,000 pieces is a financial event.

## The Golden Sample Rule (Keep One, Leave One)

After you approve the PPS, make two identical "golden samples." **Keep one at your company. Leave one at the factory.** Every bulk unit is then measured against that golden sample — it becomes the standard for the entire run.

We have seen this rule save a client real money. A buyer received a bulk shipment with a shade variation between left and right panels. Because he had left a golden sample at the factory and kept one in his office, the deviation was undeniable — the factory reworked the batch instead of arguing. Without that retained sample, "they will fix it in bulk" becomes "they say it was always like that."

> **Key point:** The golden sample is your only objective benchmark once production starts. No golden sample, no comeback.

This step sits right after [factory verification](blog-verify-chinese-factory-audit-checklist.html) in our playbook — confirm the source is real, then lock the standard so the run cannot quietly drift from it.

## Sample Costs & Timelines (Real Numbers)

Buyers routinely overestimate sample cost and use that fear to skip the step. The real ranges are manageable:

- **Sample fee:** typically $50–$300 including shipping, often 2–3× the per-unit bulk price. Per item, usually $1–$5 plus $20–$40 express.
- **Express shipping:** DHL / FedEx / UPS, 3–7 days, about $30–$80 per kilogram.
- **Refundable?** Many suppliers refund the sample fee against a bulk order — always ask, and get it in writing on the proforma invoice.

These shipping and handling costs are themselves part of your true landed cost. ## Third-Party Inspection & When to Use It

For orders above roughly $5,000, or for safety-critical and compliance-heavy products, bring in an independent inspector. Established firms (SGS, QIMA, TÜV, Intertek) run standardized checklists and issue photo-rich reports. Typical cost runs about $200–$500 per inspection — a fraction of what a bad bulk order costs. You get a photo-rich report covering quantity, workmanship, dimensions, and packaging, plus a clear pass / hold / reject call you can act on the same day.

We once had a client's PPS pass our own visual check, but an independent inspector's teardown found a substituted internal component — the kind of swap that survives a photo and fails a season later. The $280 inspection caught it before 3,000 units shipped. A proper sample evaluation at the PPS stage is exactly what makes that catch possible; an independent set of eyes finds what familiarity or optimism misses.

Use third-party inspection when:
- You cannot travel to the factory yourself
- The product is electronic, children's, or safety-relevant
- The order value justifies independent verification
- You suspect the factory's self-report may be optimistic under shipping pressure

For U.S. market requirements, the [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) publishes the safety and labeling rules that apply to many consumer goods. For the EU, the [European Commission's product safety framework (GPSR)](https://commission.europa.eu/energy-climate-change-environment/standards-and-legislation/product-safety_en) governs CE marking and general safety obligations. Check the rules for *your* destination market before the sample ships — not after.

## Red Flags in Sample Evaluation

A clean process surfaces problems early. Most of these show up during sample evaluation if you are actually looking — the right response is never anger, it is a written request for a revised sample and a dated record of the gap. Watch for these:

- **The sample has visible defects.** If the sample has flaws, the bulk order will have more. Do not accept "they will fix it in bulk."
- **"OEM grade" with no documentation.** Demand the actual certificate (IATF 16949, ISO 9001, CE, FCC). Refuse grade claims without paperwork.
- **Hesitation on the business license.** A legitimate supplier shares a clear, current 营业执照 on request. A blurry or edited copy is a red flag.
- **Specs drift between samples.** If sample #1 and sample #2 differ, your production run will vary too.
- **No tolerance offered.** A factory that cannot state its acceptable range is telling you it does not measure.

> **Key point:** Sample evaluation is also a trust test. How a supplier handles a flagged defect tells you more about your bulk order than the sample's polish does.

## Sample Evaluation FAQ

**1. Is the sample fee refundable against a bulk order?**
Often, yes — many suppliers deduct the sample fee from your first bulk order. Always ask up front and get it written into the proforma invoice. It is normal, not a favor.

**2. What is the difference between a PPS and a production sample?**
A pre-production sample is built on the actual production line with final tooling and materials — it is your last chance to change the design cheaply. A production sample is pulled from the live run to confirm the standard held. Evaluate the PPS hardest; use the production sample for confirmation only.

**3. How do I keep a golden sample correctly?**
Make two identical approved samples. Keep one in your office and leave one at the factory, clearly marked as the reference standard. Every bulk unit is then judged against it. Photograph both and date the record.

**4. What tolerance is reasonable for dimensions?**
It depends on the product, but typical acceptable ranges run about ±1–5%. Define your tolerance in the spec sheet before the sample arrives, and flag anything beyond it in writing. Tight-tolerance parts (precision gears, molded housings) need tighter limits.

**5. Do I need third-party inspection at the sample stage?**
Not always. For low-value or simple products, your own documented checklist suffices. For orders above ~$5,000, safety-critical items, or when you cannot visit the factory, an independent inspector ($200–$500) is worth it. The sample stage is also where you set the AQL levels the inspector will later apply to the bulk run.

## Conclusion: Approve With Evidence, Not Vibes

Sample evaluation is not bureaucracy — it is the cheapest insurance in sourcing. Measure against your spec, BOM-cross-check the materials, stress-test the function, verify the labeling for your market, and lock in a golden sample before you release the bulk order. Do that, and the container that shows up weeks later will match the sample on your desk.
