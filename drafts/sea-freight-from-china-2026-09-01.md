# Sea Freight from China: Complete Cost and Timeline Guide for 2026

## Why Sea Freight Still Wins for China Imports in 2026

If you are bringing products in from China, sea freight is still the default for a reason. A 40ft container moves goods at a fraction of air cost, and for most importers the math is not close. Air wins only when the shipment is small, urgent, or worth more per kilo than the freight itself.

The catch in 2026 is that "sea freight" is no longer just one price. Red Sea diversions, the EU carbon scheme, and a brutal peak season have made the final number depend heavily on your lane, your Incoterms, and how early you book. This guide walks through what a China sea shipment actually costs this year, how long it takes, and where the money leaks out.

## How Sea Freight Cost Is Built: The Full Breakdown

Most first-time importers see a single "freight rate" quote and assume that is the total. It is not. The ocean leg is the biggest line item, but the landed cost is a stack of separate charges. Here is what typically makes up a China-to-USA or China-to-Europe sea shipment in 2026.

| Cost component | Typical 2026 range | Payer under FOB | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ocean freight (main leg) | $1,200 to $7,200 per 40ft, lane dependent | Buyer | Largest variable; contract vs spot differ widely |
| Terminal handling (THC) | $150 to $350 per container | Buyer | Charged at both origin and destination |
| Documentation and customs brokerage | $100 to $400 | Buyer | ISF filing, customs bond, entry paperwork |
| Cargo insurance | 0.15% to 0.35% of cargo value | Buyer (recommended) | Cheap protection against total loss |
| Destination port charges | $200 to $600 | Buyer | Demurrage and detention are extra if slow |
| Last-mile trucking | $200 to $1,500 | Buyer | Port to your warehouse or door |
| Import duties and tariffs | 0% to 25%+ (US Section 301) | Buyer | Depends on the product HS code |

The single most common mistake is quoting only the ocean freight and forgetting duties. For US-bound goods, Section 301 tariffs can add up to 25% on top of the base duty, and that number is set by the product category, not by your freight forwarder.

A useful habit: always ask for a landed-cost estimate that includes freight, insurance, duties, and last-mile, not just the container rate. See our full breakdown in [The Hidden Costs of Importing from China](/blog/blog-hidden-costs-importing-china.html).

## FCL vs LCL: Which One Actually Saves You Money

Sea freight splits into two models. FCL (Full Container Load) means you rent the whole container. LCL (Less than Container Load) means your goods share a container with other importers and you pay by volume.

The decision is mostly about volume.

| Factor | LCL (shared container) | FCL (full container) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best for | Under about 14 to 15 CBM | 14 to 15 CBM and above |
| Pricing | $40 to $150 per CBM to the US, $80 to $180 per CBM to Europe | Flat rate per 40ft container |
| Handling | Multiple consolidations, slower | Direct, fewer touches |
| Damage risk | Higher (mixed cargo) | Lower (sealed at origin) |
| Rule of thumb | Cheaper below the breakpoint | Cheaper above the breakpoint |

The breakpoint is the key number: roughly 14 to 15 cubic meters. Below it, LCL is almost always cheaper because you are not paying for empty space. Above it, FCL wins because the flat container rate works out to less per unit than per-CBM LCL pricing.

There is a second reason to pick FCL once you cross the breakpoint: your goods are sealed at the factory and opened at your door. With LCL, your cartons sit in a warehouse with other companies' shipments, get handled several times, and face a higher chance of loss or damage. For fragile or high-value goods, that risk alone can justify a full container.

## 2026 Container Rates: China to USA and Europe

Rates in 2026 are volatile but have cooled from the 2021 to 2022 extremes. The ranges below are realistic spot levels for a 40ft container. Contract rates signed with a forwarder for regular volume typically run 10% to 25% below these spot numbers.

| Lane | 40ft FCL (2026 spot) | LCL per CBM | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| China to US West | $2,500 to $5,500 | $40 to $90 | Transpacific, not on Red Sea route |
| China to US East | $4,200 to $7,200 | $50 to $150 | Longer, Panama or Suez dependent |
| China to North Europe | $2,200 to $4,500 | $80 to $180 | Suez route, Red Sea surcharge risk |
| China to Mediterranean | $3,000 to $5,000 | $90 to $180 | Similar exposure to Suez delays |

Two things to watch. First, these are per-container rates for general cargo; hazardous goods, oversized items, and remote inland destinations cost more. Second, the spread inside each range is wide because rates move with the booking week. Booking in the quiet season (roughly November to February) can land you near the low end, while a August to October peak booking can push you past the high end.

If you ship regularly, ask your forwarder for a annual contract rate rather than paying spot each time. The savings compound across a year of shipments.

## Transit Times by Lane (and the Red Sea Detour)

Cost is only half the picture. Transit time decides how much working capital sits on the water and how fast you can restock.

| Lane | Normal transit | 2026 disruption note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transpacific to US West | 12 to 18 days | Not on Red Sea route; relatively stable |
| To US East via Panama | 25 to 35 days | Panama draft limits add occasional delays |
| To US East via Suez | 35 to 45 days | Red Sea diversion adds 10 to 14 days |
| To North Europe via Suez | 30 to 40 days | Cape of Good Hope detour adds 10 to 14 days |
| Asia-Europe rail (alternative) | 15 to 22 days | $1.50 to $4.50 per kg; faster but pricier |

The Red Sea situation is the big change of the last two years. Many carriers now route Asia-Europe services around the Cape of Good Hope instead of the Suez Canal, adding 10 to 14 days to Europe-bound transit. This does not affect Transpacific lanes to the US West, which is why those remain the most predictable.

For time-sensitive EU orders, the China-Europe rail option is worth a look. It is more expensive per kg than sea but roughly halves the transit time versus the current Suez-routed ocean service.

## Air vs Sea: The Real Break-Even Point

Air freight from China is fast but expensive, and the gap with sea has widened as ocean rates normalized. The question is not "which is better" but "at what weight does air stop making sense."

| Mode | Cost per kg | Transit time | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sea (FCL or LCL) | $0.40 to $1.50 per kg | 20 to 45 days | Bulk, non-urgent, low margin |
| Air | $4 to $12 per kg | 3 to 7 days | Urgent, high-value, small volume |
| China-EU rail | $1.50 to $4.50 per kg | 15 to 22 days | Mid-value, time-sensitive EU orders |

The practical break-even is around 300 kg, or roughly 14 to 15 CBM, depending on density. Below that, air can be competitive once you factor in the financing cost of goods sitting at sea and the risk of a stockout. Above it, sea almost always wins on pure cost.

A common pattern we see: importers air-freight the first replenishment to catch a sales window, then shift the repeat order to sea once demand is proven. That hybrid approach controls both cash flow and freight spend.

## Incoterms That Matter for Buyers: FOB, CIF, DDP

The Incoterm on your proforma invoice decides who pays for what, and it changes your freight quote meaningfully. Three come up most often for China imports.

- EXW (Ex Works): you collect goods from the factory door. Cheapest on paper, but you own every step from there, including Chinese export clearance. Best only if you have a freight partner on the ground.
- FOB (Free On Board): the supplier delivers goods onto the vessel at the Chinese port. You pay the main ocean freight, insurance, and import. This is the most common term for experienced importers because it keeps the freight under your control.
- CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight): the supplier pays freight and insurance to your destination port. Convenient, but the supplier chooses the forwarder and the rate is baked into your product price.
- DDP (Delivered Duty Paid): the supplier handles everything, including import duties, and delivers to your door. Simplest for the buyer, but you lose visibility into the true freight and duty cost. For a clear landed-cost picture, [DDP shipping from China](/landing/shipping-from-china.html) is easiest, while FOB gives you the most control.

If your goal is to compare factory quotes apples to apples, insist on FOB. If your goal is to know one number you can sell against, DDP is cleaner. Just do not confuse the two when budgeting.

## 2026 Surcharges You Cannot Ignore

The base ocean rate is only the starting point. In 2026, several surcharges routinely appear on the invoice, and ignoring them is how budgets blow up.

- Red Sea / Cape of Good Hope diversion: rerouting adds 10 to 14 days and shows up as a war-risk or reroute surcharge on Europe lanes.
- EU ETS (Emissions Trading System): from 2026 the system covers 100% of shipping emissions, adding roughly 12% to 15% to EU-bound freight cost versus the pre-ETS baseline.
- CBAM (Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism): importers of carbon-intensive goods begin paying for embedded emissions. It phases in through the decade but already affects how forwarders price EU shipments.
- Peak Season Surcharge (PSS): from August to October, carriers add 40% to 80% on top of base rates. Book early or accept the premium.
- Panama Canal restrictions: lower draft limits cut capacity on the US East route, adding delay risk during high-demand months.

The practical move is to ask for an all-in quote that lists each surcharge separately, then compare quotes on the same lane and week. A rate that looks cheap in May can look very different in September.

## Customs, Duties, and Documents You Need

Freight gets your goods to the port. Customs gets them into the country, and this is where many first shipments stall.

Core documents:
- Commercial invoice: shows value, used to assess duty.
- Packing list: shows weight and dimensions, used for handling and verification.
- Bill of Lading (B/L): the receipt for the cargo and the document of title.
- Certificate of Origin: sometimes needed for preferential duty treatment.

For the US, you also need the ISF 10+2 filing (submitted before the vessel sails) and a customs bond. Missing the ISF deadline can trigger fines and holds. For the EU, you need an EORI number and you will pay import VAT on entry, which is often recoverable if you are VAT-registered.

Duties are set by the product's HS code, not by negotiation. US Section 301 tariffs add up to 25% on a long list of Chinese-origin goods, and these sit on top of the base Most Favored Nation duty. The [China import tariff guide for 2026](/blog/blog-import-china-tariff-guide-2026.html) goes deeper on how to estimate your exposure before you buy.

A freight forwarder or customs broker earns their fee here. The cost of getting classification wrong is far higher than the brokerage fee.

## How to Cut Your China Sea-Freight Cost

You cannot control the market, but you can control how you buy. These are the levers that move the number.

1. Book early for peak season. Lock August to October space three to four weeks ahead, or accept the PSS premium.
2. Use an annual contract rate if you ship monthly. Contract pricing runs 10% to 25% below spot.
3. Cross the FCL breakpoint deliberately. If you are at 12 CBM, adding a little volume to hit a full container often costs less than LCL plus the risk.
4. Consolidate suppliers. Multiple small shipments from different factories become one container when you use a consolidator or agent with a warehouse.
5. Pre-clear customs. Have your HS codes, ISF, and bond ready before the vessel sails to avoid storage fees.
6. Compare all-in quotes, not base rates. Two quotes on the same lane can differ by the surcharges they hide.
7. Use a local agent for negotiation. A China-based agent can bundle sourcing, QC, and shipping so you are not paying three separate markups.

The biggest saving is usually structural: buying closer to the factory and consolidating volume so more of your goods travel as FCL rather than LCL. That is where a sourcing partner pays for itself.
