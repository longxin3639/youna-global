## Quick Snapshot (TL;DR)

- **On Sept 9 and Sept 10, 2026**, both sides publicly confirmed they are negotiating a **reciprocal $30 billion Section 301 tariff reduction** (about $30B in each direction).
- **This is NOT a broad rollback.** Only non-sensitive, non-strategic goods are in scope. Strategic categories (high-tech, electronics with national security concerns, semiconductors, defense-related) are explicitly out.
- **The Trump-Xi summit is set for Sept 24, 2026 in Washington** - the third face-to-face between the two leaders this year. USTR said it hopes to "publish some results around September 24."
- **The current tariff truce expires Nov 10, 2026.** Reductions are widely expected to take effect around or shortly after that date.
- **The Section 301 tariffs being targeted are the 7.5% to 25% bracket** added during the Trump administration's 2018-2019 cycle. The goal is to drop those down to MFN (Most Favored Nation) base rates or lower.

If you only have 30 seconds: watch the September 24 summit. If a deal is announced, expect details to follow in October. The actual tariff cut likely lands around November 10 when the current truce expires.

## 1. What Happened on September 9 and 10

This is a two-day news cycle, but it's a big one.

**Sept 9, 2026 (USTR side):** US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told the Financial Times News Briefing podcast that the US and China are working through a list of non-sensitive goods for reciprocal tariff reductions. His exact framing: he hoped to "publish some results around September 24" so the public can understand what is being negotiated.

**Sept 10, 2026 (Commerce Ministry side):** At China's regular Commerce Ministry press briefing, spokesperson Huang Ling confirmed the two sides are negotiating "a $30 billion reciprocal tariff reduction framework" and "hope to land it at an early date."

Why this matters: in 24 hours, both governments put concrete numbers on the table ($30B), a target date (Sept 24), and the same scope language (non-sensitive goods). This is not vague diplomatic warm-up - it is a narrowly scoped deal being telegraphed in advance. The narrowness of the scope is itself a signal: both sides want a win that they can credibly claim, without reopening the strategic-tech fight.

What we do NOT know yet:
- Which specific tariff lines are being reduced
- The exact new tariff rates (USTR said "MFN base rate or lower," but MFN itself is a number, and "lower" is a goal, not a number)
- Whether the reduction is permanent, time-limited, or conditional on continued negotiations

If you want to track these three unknowns in real time, watch the USTR Federal Register notices (search "Section 301 China" on federalregister.gov) and the Commerce Ministry press briefings (usually Thursdays, 16:00 Beijing time).

## 2. Section 301 Refresher (Why This Matters)

If you're a long-time China importer, skip this section. If you're newer or have been reading headline news without digging into the law, this is the part to read.

**Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974** is a US law that lets the President impose tariffs on imports from countries that the US Trade Representative has determined are engaging in unfair trade practices. In 2018-2019, the Trump administration used Section 301 to impose additional tariffs on a wide range of Chinese imports - the so-called "tariff war" round one.

The original 301 tariffs were imposed at rates between 7.5% and 25%, layered on top of the existing MFN base rates that China already faced. So a $10 product from China might have faced, say, 5% MFN + 25% Section 301 = 30% total US import duty in 2018-2019.

Those 301 tariffs were never fully removed under Biden or the second Trump term. They were paused, partially reduced, and selectively exempted - but the 7.5%-25% baseline stayed on the books for most categories. They are what this current negotiation is targeting.

**Why you care:** if your landed cost from China includes a 25% Section 301 line, this negotiation could directly reduce your per-unit tariff. For a $10 product, a 25% drop on the Section 301 line means $2.50 less per unit in duty. On a 5,000 unit order, that's $12,500.

Note: this is a reduction in the Section 301 ADDITION. The MFN base rate stays. So if your product was 5% MFN + 25% Section 301 = 30% total, after a deal it might be 5% MFN + 0% Section 301 = 5% total. Or it could be 5% MFN + 7.5% Section 301 = 12.5% total. We won't know the exact landing point until the official announcement.

## 3. What's Likely In and What's Likely Out

Based on reporting from USTR, the Commerce Ministry, and Barclays Bank's research note, here's the working categorization. This is NOT an official list - the official list comes with the Sept 24 announcement. Treat this as a directional preview.

**Likely in scope (subject to reciprocal reduction):**

- Low-end consumer goods: apparel, footwear, household items, toys, holiday decorations, luggage
- Light industry / daily goods: select textiles, basic home goods
- Non-critical industrial components: select parts that don't have national security implications
- Medical supplies (non-sensitive portion)
- Some energy-related non-sensitive products

**Explicitly out of scope (not in this negotiation):**

- Semiconductors and advanced chips
- Electronics with national security concerns (specific HTS codes likely excluded)
- Defense-related products
- Categories where the US has ongoing Section 232 (national security) tariffs - those are separate from Section 301
- Any category where the Biden-era or Trump-II strategic review recommended sustained tariffs

**Why the categories matter for your sourcing decisions:**

If you're sourcing in the "likely in" categories (most consumer goods, light industrial products, toys, household items), the tariff math for your next order may genuinely improve. It's reasonable to:
- Hold off on panic-restructuring your China supply chain for now
- Plan for a Q4 2026 / Q1 2027 re-quote of your landed cost
- Ask your China supplier what HS code they're classifying your product under (some buyers overpay because of classification)

If you're sourcing in the "out" categories (electronics, semiconductors, advanced tech, anything with strategic national security implications), this negotiation is largely irrelevant to your tariff picture. The status quo holds. Plan accordingly.

## 5. What Smart Importers Are Doing Right Now

This section is based on what we've seen working importers do over the past several tariff cycles. The good ones don't panic, but they don't sit still either.

**1. Confirm your HS code classification.** If your product is in the likely-in-scope categories, the classification your customs broker has been using may not match what the official reduction list will use. We've seen buyers paying 25% on products that should have been 7.5% for years. Ask your broker to re-verify your top 3 SKUs by HS code and by HTS code (they're related but different - HS is international, HTS is US-specific).

**2. Hold orders briefly if you can.** If your product is in the likely-in-scope categories and you have inventory runway, consider delaying a small portion of your next purchase order to early November. Worst case you delay some inventory; best case you land a meaningfully lower tariff. This is the cleanest arbitrage if you can afford the inventory delay.

**3. Don't restructure to Vietnam or India based on this news.** The 300-billion-dollar deal does not change the structural reality covered in our China+1 vs China-only guide - China's supply chain integration, manufacturing depth, and per-unit cost advantage remain. If you are considering China+1, do it for legitimate strategic reasons (geopolitical risk hedging, US nearshoring for specific SKUs), not because of one tariff cycle.

**4. Watch the secondary effects.** A tariff reduction usually triggers downstream changes: suppliers may renegotiate factory prices downward in response (smaller order sizes become viable), freight forwarders may adjust quotes, and US-based competitors may restock aggressively. If you're early to understand the new math, you have a window.

**5. Document your current landed cost precisely.** Before any tariff change, snapshot your exact landed cost (factory price + freight + duty + brokerage + last-mile) per SKU. This becomes the before/after comparison you actually need to make pricing decisions. "I think it got cheaper" is not actionable. "$X.XX per unit landed cost dropped from $Y.YY to $Z.ZZ" is.

## 6. How to Read the Official Announcement When It Drops

For importers who don't live and breathe trade law, the USTR and Commerce Ministry announcements can look opaque. A few practical reading tips:

**USTR Federal Register notice:** Look for the published HTS code list (usually formatted as 8-digit or 10-digit codes). Find your product's HTS code in the list. The notice will specify the new tariff rate and effective date. Cross-reference with your customs broker.

**Commerce Ministry announcement:** Usually a press release with category descriptions (not HTS codes). Easier to read but less actionable - you'll still need to translate "apparel and footwear" into the specific codes your product falls under.

**Joint statement:** Highest-level, least detail. Sets expectations but doesn't have the operational specifics. Useful for understanding the political framing but not for compliance.

**HTS code tools:** The US International Trade Commission maintains a free HTS search tool at hts.usitc.gov. Search your product description, find the matching code, then look for that code in any published USTR list.
